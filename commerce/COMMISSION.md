# How the money actually comes in

Three routes, three different words for "commission", and only one of them is
what most people mean by dropshipping. They are not interchangeable, and the
arithmetic differs enough that picking the wrong one quietly loses money.

| Route | What you do | What you get | Where the money is thin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Resell** | Take the supplier's product file, set your own retail price, keep the difference | Margin — retail minus trade, minus carriage, fees, returns | Commodity homeware margins are single-digit percent unless you add something |
| **List on a marketplace** | Send products to The Range, eBay, OnBuy, Amazon; they bring the shopper, you fulfil | Revenue minus a commission of roughly 5–20% | You do not own the customer, and the commission is charged on delivery too |
| **Refer** | Write pages that send shoppers to a retailer with your affiliate link | A commission of roughly 5–10% of the sale | You have to build the traffic first; a click that buys nothing earns nothing |

Fees and rates below were checked in November 2026 and move. Every figure is
linked so you can check it again before you price anything.

## 1. Reselling: the margin, worked out honestly

The demo product in this repo is a bamboo shelf at **£49.99**. Take plausible
trade numbers for a UK homeware dropshipper and follow the £49.99 to the bottom.

| Line | Amount | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retail price (VAT inclusive) | £49.99 | What the shopper pays |
| Less VAT at 20% | −£8.33 | You collect this for HMRC; it was never yours |
| **Net revenue** | **£41.66** | |
| Trade cost of the goods | −£32.00 | A typical trade price is 60–70% of a supplier's RRP |
| Carriage to the customer | −£4.50 | "Free delivery" is free to the shopper, not to you |
| Card / wallet fees (≈1.5% + 20p) | −£0.82 | |
| Returns provision (4% of orders, ~£12 each all-in) | −£0.50 | Resealed goods, return postage, the occasional write-off |
| **Left over** | **£3.84** | **9.2% of net revenue, 7.7% of the retail price** |

That £3.84 has to pay for advertising, the site, and your time. At a £0.60
cost-per-click and a 3% conversion rate, one sale costs about £20 in ads — which
is **five times the entire margin**. That is the trap: the maths works only if
traffic is free (search, social, a list) or the margin is much fatter.

Two ways to make the number bigger:

- **Sell something with a real markup.** Measured across 221 real dropshipping
  products, the median markup is about **4×** cost, and home goods average around
  **5×** ([productlair](https://productlair.com/blog/how-to-price-dropshipping-products)).
  A £32 trade item priced at 4× is £128, not £49.99. The supplier's RRP is a
  suggestion aimed at a big retailer's cost base, not yours.
- **Sell a range, not a product.** Bundles, a set, a gift box, your own
  photography and copy. The margin comes from the shopping experience, because it
  cannot come from the goods.

Published UK benchmarks put dropshipping **net** margins at roughly **5–15%**, with
gross margins of 15–30% before ads
([sellerbookkeeping](https://sellerbookkeeping.uk/ecommerce-profit-calculator/),
[thesecondincome](https://thesecondincome.uk/articles/dropshipping-uk-guide.html)).
If a supplier's "commission" sounds like 30%, that is usually a markup you are
expected to apply, not a rebate you receive.

**VAT, before you do any of this.** The UK registration threshold is **£90,000**
of turnover in a rolling 12 months. Below it you are not charging VAT — which
means your prices are 20% cheaper than a registered competitor's, and you cannot
reclaim the VAT on your costs. Above it, every price in the example above
changes. Model both before choosing a price.

## 2. Marketplaces: commission, and who owns the customer

You keep your own stock (or your supplier's), list on someone else's shop, and
pay a percentage. Useful when you have products and no traffic; expensive when
you have both.

| Where | Commission | Fixed costs | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [The Range Marketplace](https://marketplace.therange.co.uk/) | **11–15%** of the total including delivery: 11% toys and pets, 13% furniture, home and lighting, 14% garden, 15% cooking, dining and clothing | None — no monthly fee, no listing fee | "No sale, no fee". Commission may not be returned when an order is refunded; 90 days' notice of fee changes. Service standards: 98% despatched on time, under 1% cancelled for stock, late delivery penalty 10–30% of order value ([detail](https://e-tailize.com/integrations/selling-on-the-range/), [guide](https://www.plusranger.co.uk/blog/selling-on-the-range-marketplace/)) |
| [eBay UK](https://www.ebay.co.uk) business seller | **11.9%** home, furniture and DIY up to £500 (7.9% above), **10.9%** garden and patio, toys, baby, health and beauty | 30p per order (40p over £10) + 0.35% regulatory fee, all plus VAT | Fees are on the total including postage ([rates](https://getprivy.co.uk/articles/ebay-fees-uk)) |
| [Amazon UK](https://sell.amazon.co.uk) | **8–15%** referral by category | £25/month pro account (excl. VAT), plus fulfilment if you use FBA | Largest demand, most competition, and you are a guest in someone's shop |
| [OnBuy](https://www.onbuy.com) | Quoted as **5–9%** by its own marketing; a 2026 review reports a **15% standard** with category exceptions | From £19–25/month, waived in some months under £500 of sales | Conflicting published numbers — get the current fee schedule in writing before pricing ([one source](https://marqetir.com/marketplaces/onbuy), [another](https://www.livebusinessblog.co.uk/why-is-onbuy-so-cheap/)) |

Marketplace commission is charged on the **total including delivery**, so "free
delivery" costs you twice: once in postage and once in commission on the postage
you did not charge. Work the percentage into the list price, not out of the
margin afterwards.

## 3. Referring: affiliate commission, which is the word most people actually mean

Here you do not hold stock, take payment or handle returns. You write the pages,
send the click, and get paid a percentage when it converts. It is the only one of
the three where **you** are the one being paid a commission.

| Programme | Rate | Cookie | Paid | Catch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Aosom UK affiliate](https://www.aosom.co.uk/page/business) (via Awin) | **6–10%** by category: 8% garden and outdoor, 8% home, 8% pet, 7% DIY, 7% health and beauty, 10% toys, 10% sports | 30 days | Monthly, through Awin | About £150 average order value; £25 bonus at 50 sales, £50 at 100 ([rates](https://commission.academy/blog/best-garden-shed-affiliate-programs/), [Awin listing](https://ui.awin.com/merchant-profile/17151)) |
| The Range | Affiliate programme runs through the usual networks; rates vary by network and season | Network default | Network cycle | Check the current rate in the network, not on a blog |
| BigBuy, Avasam, Syncee, Spocket | Referral programmes for signing up other sellers, not for selling products | — | — | Small, one-off, and not a business |

Realistic affiliate arithmetic: a 30-day cookie, a 3% conversion rate and an £80
average order means **100 clicks ≈ 3 sales ≈ £19.20** at 8%. EPC (earnings per
click) for Aosom UK is quoted around **£0.39**, which is why affiliate income is
a content business: the money is in the pages that rank, not in the links.

**Why this matters for this repository.** The catalogue builder already produces
the two things an affiliate site needs: crawlable pages with real product facts,
and a validated Merchant feed. A "shop" with no checkout, honest about it, is a
legitimate affiliate asset — and it is the cheapest way to find out which
products people actually want before you buy stock or take on a feed.

## Which one to pick

| Your situation | Route | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No audience, no stock, £200 to spend | Affiliate pages first, on a narrow niche | No cash risk, and it tells you what sells |
| A niche audience already (a club, a following, a local trade) | Resell a short range | You can charge for curation instead of competing on price |
| Products, no traffic | Marketplace, starting with the lowest commission in your category | Someone else's shoppers, commission you can see |
| Products and traffic | Your own shop, marketplace as a second channel | Keep the margin where you earned it |
| A supplier offering "30% commission" and no paperwork | Walk away | Real trade terms are on a price list, not a promise |

## The traps worth naming

- **"Dropshipping academies"** that sell courses and a "done-for-you store" for a
  monthly fee. The fee is their business; your shop is the product.
- **Commission that only pays on your own recruits.** That is a pyramid with an
  ecommerce vocabulary.
- **Cashback and coupon sites** as your only traffic: they take the commission
  you were counting on, and the customer was going to buy anyway.
- **Free returns with a 15% commission platform.** Two costs on one order, and
  the commission may not come back when the order does.
- **A supplier's RRP.** It is set for a retailer with different costs. Price from
  your own numbers.

## A 30-day way to start, with the files in this repo

1. **Week 1 — accounts.** Apply to Aosom B2B (or Avasam if you want a directory
   first), a Stripe account, and one marketplace where your category's commission
   is lowest. Nothing is live yet.
2. **Week 2 — download, don't retype.**
   ```bash
   cd commerce
   npm run feed -- --url "<the feed URL from your supplier portal>" --name supplier
   npm run catalogue -- feeds/supplier-<date>.csv
   ```
   Read the issues list. Every "no GTIN", "no image" and "repaired" line is a
   product that would have failed silently later.
3. **Week 3 — price and rewrite.** Put your own numbers in, apply the margin
   arithmetic above, and rewrite the first paragraph of every description. The
   supplier's copy is on fifty other shops.
4. **Week 4 — decide, with evidence.** Publish the catalogue, submit the Merchant
   feed, and watch which pages get clicks. Only then decide whether to take the
   marketplace commission, buy stock, or stay affiliate.

Nothing in this repository takes a payment. `GAPS.md` says what has to be built
before it can, and in what order.
